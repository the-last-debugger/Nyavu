How Nyavu was measured

One held-out set, split by registrable domain, reported without rounding up. Every number the interface quotes is produced by this page's method, and the mistakes are shown too.

The corpus

17,730 addresses, 8,865 labelled phishing and 8,865 labelled benign, drawn from 16,185 registrable domains. The classes are balanced by construction, not by chance, so accuracy is not an artefact of a lopsided sample. Benign addresses come from Tranco (8,865); phishing addresses come from Phishing.Database (8,736) and OpenPhish (129).

The split, and why it is grouped

Train and test are split by registrable domain, not by URL, with a fixed seed. A campaign puts many near-identical URLs on one domain; splitting by URL would put the same domain on both sides and report a score that comes from memory rather than from reading.

Does the number mean what it says?

Read the ends honestly: below 0.2 the model is under-confident, predicting about 6 in 100 where 10 in 100 were phishing, and about 15 where 22 were. Near the top it is close. That is the safer direction to be wrong in for a detector, since it under-states risk rather than inventing it.

Where the bands come from

The verdict threshold is 50: at or above it the card says phishing. The bands around that line are for reading the score, not for changing the decision. They sit 15 points either side so a score near the middle is shown as uncertain rather than pushed to one side. The sweep is the evidence for that choice.

What it gets wrong

  • False positive http://marpple.shop

    Labelled benign by Tranco, scored 96.8 in 100. The name is short and ends in .shop, a cheap ending the model has learned to distrust, and the reputation list does not reach this domain.

    Measured: cheap ending, length 12.

  • False positive http://telegram.im

    Labelled benign by Tranco, scored 95.6 in 100. This is Telegram's own .im address. The character patterns of the name resemble the brand-imitating domains in the corpus, and there is no institution or reputation entry to override the score.

    Measured: length 11.

  • False negative http://doxa.rs

    Labelled phishing by Phishing.Database, scored 3.3 in 100. Four letters, no digits, no hyphens, no brand: there is almost nothing in the name for the model to read, which is the case it is weakest at.

    Measured: length 7.

  • False negative https://mobilefb-vvme9wil.88live.tv

    Labelled phishing by Phishing.Database, scored 3.5 in 100. The brand sits in a subdomain, but the model reads only the registered domain, so the imitation is invisible to it and the host looks like a hosting suffix.

    Measured: 2 digits, length 9.

These are the highest-confidence errors in the held-out set, not curated examples: two benign addresses the model was most sure about, and two phishing addresses it was most sure were fine. They are the fastest way to see the shape of its blind spots.

What it cannot see

  • Paths and queries. Only the registered domain and its shape are read. A clean domain with a hostile path is invisible to the model, though the interface's credential rule catches some.
  • Registration and reputation. There is no WHOIS, no age, no certificate data. The reputation signal is a Tranco top-1,200 list and an institution list of published addresses.
  • Page content. Nothing is fetched, so what the page would have shown is unknown.
  • Time. The corpus has a date. A domain re-registered after it was labelled can change class, which is why a report is dated.
  • A miss never clears an address. Absence from a list is not evidence of safety, and the card says so.

Artefacts: ml/artifacts/metrics.json (all three models, cross-validation, threshold sweep), ml/artifacts/holdout_predictions.csv (per-URL probabilities used for the curve and the matrix), data/processed/urls_v2.csv (the corpus).
